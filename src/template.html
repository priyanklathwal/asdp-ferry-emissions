Sunda Strait Ferry Emissions

What the Sunda Strait ferries burn

Every twelve minutes or so, a ferry leaves Merak for Bakauheni. It is fifteen nautical miles. Here is what that costs in energy, in carbon, and in the air over two Indonesian port towns, and what would actually change it.

CONTEXT

Indonesia's busiest ferry crossing, and what it would take to clean it up

Java and Sumatra, the two most populous islands in Indonesia, are joined by a fifteen-mile stretch of water called the Sunda Strait. Almost everything that moves between them by road crosses it on a ferry, from the port of Merak on the Java side to Bakauheni on Sumatra. About 170 sailings a day, around the clock, carrying trucks, buses, cars and passengers. It is one of the busiest ferry corridors in the world.

The ferries burn marine diesel and heavy fuel oil. This briefing asks three questions a policymaker would want answered before committing money to cleaning the crossing up. How much fuel and carbon does it actually account for? Where in the operation does the energy go? And of the options on the table, from blending in biofuel to replacing the fleet with battery-powered ships, which ones would cut emissions, at what cost, and under what conditions?

The answers are built from seven months of ship-movement records and a register of the vessels involved, using the same method the International Maritime Organization uses for its global inventories. Every number here is an estimate, and the tabs on methodology and data say how good each one is.

    WHERE THE ENERGY GOES

    Hotel load, not propulsion, is where the energy goes: these ships spend most of their day stationary with the generators running

    The crossing is about 15 nautical miles. Observed port-to-port times cluster around three hours, which implies an average speed well below the design speed of these hulls. Part of that is genuine slow steaming; part is queueing offshore for a berth, which the movement data cannot separate from sailing. The model treats it as low-load steaming with a floor on propulsion power, and the size of that floor is the largest single uncertainty here.

    IMPLICATIONS

    What follows for policy

    Four conclusions fall out of the numbers, and they point in a different direction from the one most fleet-decarbonisation conversations start in.

    1. Treat the ferries as a power-sector project. The cheapest way to run this corridor is on electricity, by a wide margin. Whether that also cuts emissions depends entirely on how the electricity is made. At today's grid mix it does not. Any electrification plan for Merak and Bakauheni should be written together with a plan for the generation that feeds it, most plausibly solar and storage at the two terminals, where the operator is already building large arrays for terminal load.
    2. Start with the ships that are not moving. More than a quarter of the corridor's energy is drawn by vessels sitting alongside or queueing offshore with their generators running. Shore power at the berths, with a connection for the anchorage, reaches that block without touching a single engine, saves the operator money from the first year, and becomes a clean-energy measure the moment the supply is clean. It is also the enabling infrastructure for any later battery fleet.
    3. Use biofuel for the emissions that cannot wait. Blending renewable diesel into the existing fleet is the only measure here that cuts emissions this year with no new hardware. It costs more per litre and its abatement cost sits at the high end of what shipping measures usually cost, but it works with the vessels that exist. Indonesia already mandates a 40% biodiesel blend on land; the question is whether the same feedstock can be spared for the strait.
    4. Fix the data before spending on the fleet. The vessel list supplied for this study turned out to cover a different set of ships from the ones actually working the corridor, and it carries no engine power. A replacement programme priced against that list would be priced against the wrong fleet. Three inexpensive data requests, described on the methodology tab, would remove most of the uncertainty in every figure on this page.

    None of this argues against electrifying the corridor. It argues for sequencing: clean the stationary load and the power supply first, buy the batteries second, and blend biofuel in the meantime. Done in that order, each step is cheaper than the last one made it look.

    THE DECIDING NUMBER

    This is the argument for treating ferry electrification as a power-sector project rather than a shipping project. The vessels are a captive, highly predictable load at two fixed points, which is close to ideal for a dedicated solar-plus-storage build at the terminals. ASDP is already installing large solar arrays at both ports for terminal load. The question worth putting to them is whether that programme can be sized against a charging load instead.

    METHODOLOGY

    How the numbers were built

    The approach is the one the International Maritime Organization uses for its global greenhouse gas inventories, applied to one corridor. It works from the bottom up: take each ship, work out how hard its engines were working for how long, convert that to fuel, and convert fuel to emissions using published factors. The parameter tables come from the International Council on Clean Transportation's documentation of that method.

    1
    Movements. Every recorded departure and arrival between the two ports, 33,695 of them over 200 days, gives each crossing's duration. Time alongside comes from the gap before the same ship's next departure.
    2
    Phases. Each crossing is split into manoeuvring, steaming, and waiting for a berth. A ship's own fastest crossings define how long the passage takes when it is not queueing; anything longer is treated as waiting with main engines off, the IMO convention for a ship at anchor.
    3
    Power. The register has no engine power, so propulsion power is estimated from each hull's size and observed speed using a standard naval-architecture relation, with published corrections for hull fouling, weather and draught. Hotel and ventilation load comes from the IMO's published figure for ro-pax ferries.
    4
    Emissions. Engine work in kilowatt-hours is multiplied by published emission factors for carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, black carbon and the local air pollutants, with the IMO's penalty for engines running at low load. Fuel mass and fuel energy are recovered from the carbon dioxide.
    5
    Options. Each fuel-switching pathway is costed by holding the useful energy constant and changing what supplies it: a different fuel and its price, or electricity at the industrial tariff and the grid's carbon intensity.

    Two checks keep this honest. Every input that is not read from the data is tagged as either transcribed from a published source or an analyst's assumption, and listed on the data tab. And nine model outputs are compared against figures known independently, from engine manufacturers' data to the published ferry timetable; all nine currently agree.

    SENSITIVITY

    Modelling choices now move the answer by under 7%; the remaining uncertainty is missing data, not method

    Resolving the phase structure against the vessels' own fastest crossings, rather than imposing an arbitrary floor on engine load, has cut the structural spread from about ±15% to under 7%. What remains is a data gap, not a modelling choice: installed engine power is absent from the register, and positional AIS would replace the phase inference entirely.
